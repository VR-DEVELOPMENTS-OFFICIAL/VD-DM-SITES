Free Home Tiffin Starter Checklist

If you cook well at home, a small tiffin service can be a way to earn from your kitchen. This guide takes you from a simple menu to a trial lunch, a clean kitchen, a price that covers your costs, and your first customers. It is free, and it is not a promise of income.

Before you start

Check the food licence rules first. In India, selling cooked food to the public generally needs registration or a licence from FSSAI (the food safety authority), even for a small home kitchen. Which one you need depends on the size of your business and your state. Ask your local food safety office or look up the FSSAI registration steps on its official website before you grow beyond a trial. Also check any rules of your housing society and local municipality.

Start small. Use your existing kitchen and pots. Do not buy expensive equipment or borrow money to start. Nobody can tell you in advance how many customers you will get.

Always ask every customer about food allergies and special needs, and never promise a dish is "healthy", "pure" or "allergy-free" unless you are sure it is true.

Step 1. Plan a simple menu

Keep the menu small. A few dishes you can cook well and the same way every day are better than a long menu that is hard to manage. Start with one or two meal types, for example a lunch box.

  • Choose one meal: a lunch box is the easiest to start with.
  • Choose a fixed plan: for example rice, dal or sambar, one vegetable curry, curd or pickle, and roti or an extra item on some days.
  • Rotate the menu by day: write a simple weekly plan so customers know what to expect and you can shop in advance.
  • Use food that travels well: dishes that do not turn watery or cold quickly inside a box.
  • Make one fixed portion size: use the same spoon or cup every time, so cost and quantity stay the same.

Write the menu on one page with the price and the time customers must order by. A photo of the box helps.

Step 2. Work out the cost and price of one meal

Add up what one meal costs you: ingredients, gas and packing. Then choose a price that is above that cost and still fair for customers in your area. Compare with other tiffin services near you before you decide.

Cost per meal = ingredients + gas + packing
Profit per meal = price - cost per meal

Example only: one meal costs ₹50 and you sell it at ₹80. That leaves ₹30 on each meal. If you sold 10 meals a day for 26 days, that would be 10 × 26 × ₹30 = ₹7,800 a month after costs. Your costs, prices and number of customers will be different, and some days may be slow. This is not an income promise.

Step 3. Run a trial lunch for 10 people

Before you ask anyone to pay every day, cook a trial lunch for 10 people you know, such as neighbours, friends and family. Tell them clearly that it is a trial and ask for honest feedback. Give each person the same meal in the box you plan to use.

  1. Pick one day and cook 10 portions using the exact recipe and portion size you will use every day.
  2. Time yourself: how long does it take to cook, pack and hand over 10 boxes?
  3. Note your real cost for the day and compare it with your calculator.
  4. Hand over the boxes on time. Keep the food hot, and tell people to eat it the same day.
  5. Ask for feedback the same day or the next morning. Write the answers down.

Step 5. Check licence rules before you grow

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Rules differ by state and by the size of your business. Before you take regular paid orders or grow to more customers, find out whether you need FSSAI registration or a licence, and follow the rules that apply to you.

  1. Search for the FSSAI registration steps on the official FSSAI website, or visit your local food safety office and ask what a small home kitchen needs.
  2. Ask your housing society whether cooking for sale and deliveries are allowed in your building.
  3. Check if your municipality or local body has any rules for food sold from homes.
  4. Keep your registration or licence number, once you have it, on your menu and boxes where the rules ask for it.
  5. If you are not sure, ask a local food business owner or an accountant for help. It is better to ask first than to be told to stop later.

Step 6. Find your first customers

Start with a small number of regular customers you can serve well. Ten happy regulars are better than thirty customers you cannot keep up with.

  • Neighbours: offer the trial lunch to people in your building or street. Ask if they know anyone else who needs a meal.
  • Office groups: ask a friend who works in an office near you to share your menu with colleagues. Office workers often want a regular lunch box.
  • WhatsApp: post your menu with price, order time and delivery area in your status. Create a small broadcast list for people who ask to hear about the next day's menu.
  • Students and workers nearby: a hostel, PG or small shop near you may need daily meals. Ask politely, with the menu in hand.

Take advance payment for a week or a month by UPI, once you are sure you can deliver. Keep a simple notebook with each customer's name, address, orders and payment.
